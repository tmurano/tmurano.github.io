Research

1. Neuronal hyperactivity, neuronal maturation state and antidepressant effects

Mature neurons are not fixed in their state: granule cells of the hippocampal dentate gyrus can return to an immature-like state in adulthood (“dematuration”; Mol Brain 2019). Using repeated optogenetic stimulation of the dentate gyrus (REPOPS) that models electroconvulsive therapy, we showed that repetitive neuronal activation regulates the maturation state of neurons via nuclear reprogramming, together with antidepressant-like behavioural changes (Nat Commun 2026). Building on this, we now study why repeated neuronal hyperactivity has antidepressant effects, and how neuronal hyperactivity contributes to the pathophysiology of psychiatric disorders, using single-nucleus transcriptomics and epigenomics (supported by JST FOREST and JSPS KAKENHI).

2. Transcriptome analysis and meta-analysis of human brain disorders

We have consistently analysed public gene-expression data from human post-mortem brains and disease models across studies, to find molecular changes shared by psychiatric and neurological disorders: immature-like gene expression in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex of patients with alcoholism (Sci Rep 2017), transcriptomic immaturity induced by neuronal hyperexcitation that is shared by multiple neuropsychiatric disorders (Commun Biol 2019), and gene expression linked to brain pH across disorders (Front Psychiatry 2023). Along this line we develop methods that score gene expression against external reference signatures; RFind-sc extends this approach to single cells (bioRxiv 2026; see Software).

3. Information coding in the brain: calcium imaging

We are interested in how information is encoded by populations of neurons, especially in the dentate gyrus and in the dentate gyrus of disease models. With calcium imaging in freely moving mice and machine learning, we showed that multiple types of navigational information — position, speed, and direction of motion — are independently encoded in the population activity of dentate gyrus neurons, and that decoding of some of this information is selectively reduced in αCaMKII heterozygous knockout mice, which show deficits in spatial memory (PNAS 2022).
